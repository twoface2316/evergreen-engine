Same fabric, different weights

Garden stores sell "row cover," "frost cloth," "frost blanket," and "garden fabric" as if they were different products. They're all the same kind of material: a thin, spunbonded fabric that lets water and some light through while trapping heat rising from the soil. What changes is the weight, listed on the package in ounces per square yard (oz/yd²). Lighter fabric is usually sold as {{gear:row-cover|row cover}}; heavier fabric as {{gear:frost-cloth|frost cloth}}.

What each weight does

Utah State University Extension gives these typical figures:

0.5–0.55 oz/yd²85%28°FAll-purpose: light frosts, early spring planting, season extension
0.9–2.0 oz/yd²30–50%24–28°FFrost nights and cold snaps; remove or vent during the day

University of Wisconsin–Madison Extension notes that heavyweight covers give 4–8°F of freeze protection, and that they're the ones durable enough to reuse for several seasons.

Which one to buy

  • One roll for most gardens: a medium weight around 0.5–0.9 oz/yd². Light enough to leave on cool-season crops for days at a time, heavy enough for an ordinary frost night.
  • For protecting tomatoes, peppers, and other tender plants on frost nights: a heavy frost cloth (1.5–2.0 oz/yd²). It blocks too much light to leave on for long, so cover in the evening and uncover in the morning.
  • For pests, not cold: the lightest insect-barrier weight. It keeps cabbage worms and squash pests off without overheating plants in summer.

On a hard-freeze night you can also double up a lighter fabric for extra protection, so one roll can cover both jobs in a pinch.

How much to buy

Fabric laid directly over plants needs to reach the ground on both sides and have extra to hold down. For width, measure:

bed width + (plant height × 2) + 2 feet

A 4-foot-wide bed of 2-foot-tall plants needs fabric about 10 feet wide. For length, add 2 feet to the bed length so the ends can be tucked and weighted. Over hoops, measure the arc of one hoop from ground to ground and add 2 feet.

Row cover is sold in many widths and lengths. Buying one wide roll and cutting it to size is usually cheaper than several small packs.

Hoops, clips, and weights

  • Direct over plants works for sturdy, low crops like greens and carrots. Leave slack so plants can grow.
  • Over hoops is better for tall or tender plants and for anything you'll cover for weeks. {{gear:hoops|Wire or fiberglass hoops}} every 3–4 feet hold the fabric off the leaves, where frost damage happens on contact. {{gear:snap-clamps|Snap clamps}} keep fabric on the hoops in wind.
  • Hold the edges down with soil, boards, rocks, or sandbags every few feet. Utah State Extension suggests weights about every 3 feet. Pins and staples can tear lightweight fabric.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Covering too late. Put covers on before sunset so they trap the day's soil heat. See how to protect plants from frost.
  • Plastic touching leaves. Plastic sheeting conducts cold; leaves touching it can freeze. Use fabric, or keep plastic on hoops off the plants.
  • Leaving heavy covers on in sun. Heavy fabric and plastic can overheat plants on a sunny day. Vent or remove them once it's above freezing.
  • Forgetting pollinators. Squash, cucumbers, and melons need bees. Remove covers once they start to flower.
