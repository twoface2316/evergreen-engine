Count backward from your first fall frost

Spring planting counts forward from the last frost. Fall planting runs the other way: start from your typical first fall frost and work backward to find the last date you can plant and still harvest.

The basic calculation for any crop:

  • Start with the crop's days to maturity from the seed packet.
  • Add about two weeks as a "fall factor." Days get shorter and cooler as fall goes on, and plants grow more slowly than the packet's summer-based estimate.
  • Add time to harvest. Many crops, like greens and roots, can be picked over several weeks.
  • Count that many days back from your first frost date.

Cool-season crops that tolerate frost, such as kale, spinach, carrots, and cabbage, don't have to be finished by the first frost. A light frost often improves their flavor, and they can keep growing until a hard freeze. That buys extra weeks for crops that tolerate cold.

Best crops for fall

  • Leafy greens: lettuce, spinach, arugula, kale, and Swiss chard. Spinach and kale can carry on into early winter with a little protection.
  • Root crops: carrots, beets, radishes, and turnips. Carrots left in the ground through a few frosts turn noticeably sweeter.
  • Brassicas: broccoli, cauliflower, cabbage, and Brussels sprouts. In many regions the fall crop does better than the spring one, because heads mature in cool weather instead of bolting in summer heat.
  • Garlic: planted in fall, harvested the next summer. Cloves go in a few weeks before the first frost so roots can establish before the ground freezes.
  • Herbs: cilantro bolts quickly in summer heat but thrives in fall.

Getting fall crops started in summer heat

The catch with fall gardening is that you plant during the hottest part of summer. A few habits help:

  • Keep seedbeds moist. Small seeds can dry out in a single hot afternoon. Water lightly every day until seedlings emerge, or lay a board or burlap over the row to hold moisture (remove it as soon as sprouts appear).
  • Give some shade. Shade cloth or the shadow of taller crops protects young seedlings during heat waves.
  • Sow a little deeper than in spring, where soil stays cooler and damper.
  • Lettuce and spinach germinate poorly in hot soil. Sow them in the evening, or start them indoors and transplant.

Stretching the fall season

The first fall frost is often followed by weeks of mild weather. {{gear:row-cover|Row covers}}, {{gear:hoops|low tunnels}}, and {{gear:cold-frame|cold frames}} keep greens and roots producing well past it.
