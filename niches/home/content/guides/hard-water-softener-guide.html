What hardness measures

Hardness is the amount of dissolved calcium and magnesium in water, picked up as it moves through limestone, chalk and other rock. It's reported in milligrams per liter (mg/L) as calcium carbonate, or in grains per gallon (gpg); one grain per gallon equals 17.1 mg/L. The US Geological Survey's bands:

  • Soft: 0–60 mg/L (under 3.5 gpg)
  • Moderately hard: 61–120 mg/L (3.5–7 gpg)
  • Hard: 121–180 mg/L (7–10.5 gpg)
  • Very hard: above 180 mg/L (over 10.5 gpg)

Is hard water bad for you?

No. Calcium and magnesium are nutrients, and the World Health Organization sets no health-based limit for hardness. The problems are practical:

  • Scale inside water heaters, which wastes energy and shortens their life; scale in kettles, coffee makers, dishwashers and washing machines.
  • Spots on dishes and glass, and white crust on faucets and showerheads.
  • Soap and shampoo lather poorly, so you use more, and laundry can feel stiff.

Below about 7 gpg, most households live with it. Above 10, a softener usually pays for itself in appliance life, energy and soap.

Softeners vs "salt-free" conditioners

A water softener uses ion exchange: resin beads swap the calcium and magnesium for sodium (or potassium). It's the only common home system that actually removes hardness. It needs periodic regeneration with salt and drains some water when it does.

Softening adds a little sodium — roughly 8 mg per liter for each grain per gallon removed. For most people that's minor next to food, but anyone on a strict low-sodium diet can use potassium chloride instead of salt, or keep an unsoftened tap for drinking.

Salt-free conditioners (often "template-assisted crystallization") don't remove minerals; they change them so they're less likely to stick as scale. They need no salt or drain and can reduce scale in heaters and pipes, but they won't stop spotting or improve lathering. Magnetic and electronic "descalers" have little independent evidence behind them.

What size softener you need

A softener's rating in grains is how much hardness it removes between regenerations. To size one: people in the home × about 75 gallons per person per day × your hardness in gpg = grains per day. Multiply by about 7 days between regenerations and round up to a standard size (24,000, 32,000, 48,000 grains and so on). Add about 1 gpg for every 1 ppm of iron.

Buying tips

  • Choose a metered, demand-initiated unit, which regenerates based on actual water use rather than a timer; it uses much less salt.
  • Look for certification to NSF/ANSI Standard 44 for residential softeners.
  • Soften the water going to the water heater and indoor fixtures; outdoor spigots usually don't need it.
  • A softener doesn't filter contaminants like lead or PFAS — pair it with a certified drinking-water filter if your report calls for one.
