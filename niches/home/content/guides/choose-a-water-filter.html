Start with what you want to remove

A filter is only as good as its match to your water. Before buying, check your city's page on this site and your utility's water quality report for violations and detected contaminants. Common reasons to filter:

  • Taste and odor, usually chlorine or chloramine used for disinfection.
  • Lead, from older pipes and fixtures — the most common health reason. See our lead guide.
  • Disinfection byproducts (trihalomethanes and haloacetic acids), among the most frequent health-based violations nationwide.
  • PFAS ("forever chemicals"). See our PFAS guide.
  • Nitrate or arsenic, mostly a concern for private wells and some small systems.

Read the certification, not the box

"Removes 99% of contaminants" means nothing on its own. Look for certification by an accredited body (NSF, IAPMO, WQA or CSA) to a specific standard, and check that the specific contaminant you care about is on the certified list:

  • NSF/ANSI 42 — aesthetic effects: chlorine taste and odor, particles. Many cheap filters are certified only to this.
  • NSF/ANSI 53 — health effects: lead, cysts, many volatile organic chemicals, and (when listed) PFOA and PFOS.
  • NSF/ANSI 58 — reverse osmosis systems, which remove a broad range including arsenic, nitrate, fluoride, PFAS and dissolved solids.
  • NSF/ANSI 401 — "emerging" contaminants such as some pharmaceuticals and pesticides.
  • NSF/ANSI 55 — ultraviolet disinfection, mainly for well water with bacteria concerns.

Certification lists are public: search the certifier's online database by brand and model to see exactly what a filter is certified to reduce.

Types of filters

  • Pitchers and dispensers — cheapest and no installation. Some are certified for lead and PFOA/PFOS under Standard 53; many are certified only for taste. Slow, small capacity, frequent cartridge changes. Example: an {{amazon:NSF 53 certified water filter pitcher lead|NSF 53-certified pitcher}}.
  • Faucet-mount filters — inexpensive, filter on demand, and several are certified for lead.
  • Under-sink carbon filters — larger cartridges, higher flow, a dedicated faucet; good all-round choice for lead, chlorine taste and byproducts when certified to 42 and 53. Example: {{amazon:under sink water filter NSF 53|under-sink filters}}.
  • Reverse osmosis (RO) — the broadest removal, including PFAS, nitrate and arsenic. Costs more, wastes some water, and removes minerals; usually installed under the sink. Example: {{amazon:reverse osmosis system NSF 58|NSF 58 reverse osmosis systems}}.
  • Whole-house filters — treat all water entering the home, mainly for chlorine taste and sediment. Most don't target lead, which can come from pipes after the filter.

Keep it working

An expired cartridge can stop removing contaminants and even release what it trapped. Replace cartridges on schedule — typically every two to six months for pitchers and faucet filters, six to twelve months for under-sink carbon, with RO membranes lasting longer. Certified filters are tested for a rated capacity in gallons; heavy use means changing sooner.

Filters vs softeners

A water softener removes hardness minerals but not lead, PFAS or byproducts, and a carbon filter doesn't remove hardness. If you have very hard water and a health concern, you may need both — see our hard water guide.
