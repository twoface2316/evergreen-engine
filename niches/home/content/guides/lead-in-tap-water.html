Where lead comes from

Lakes, rivers and aquifers rarely contain much lead. It gets into tap water on the way to your faucet, as water sits in contact with:

  • Lead service lines — the pipe from the water main to your house. Millions are still in the ground, mostly in homes built before the 1950s and especially in older Midwestern and Northeastern cities.
  • Lead solder on copper pipes, banned for drinking water in 1986.
  • Brass faucets and fittings, which until 2014 could contain up to 8% lead.

Water that is corrosive — acidic, or low in minerals — pulls more lead from these materials, which is why utilities add corrosion control treatment. EPA's health goal for lead is zero: there is no known safe level, and children and pregnant women are most at risk.

What your utility's lead number means

Utilities test tap water in a sample of homes chosen because they're most likely to have lead plumbing, then report the 90th-percentile result: 90% of the sampled homes were at or below that number. If it exceeds the action level, the utility must improve corrosion control, educate customers and replace lead lines.

The action level is 15 parts per billion (ppb) today. Under EPA's Lead and Copper Rule Improvements, it drops to 10 ppb on November 1, 2027, and most utilities must replace every lead service line within 10 years after that. EPA has defended the rule in court, and states are adopting it ahead of the 2027 start.

Of the {{leadSystems}} main utilities on this site with lead results, {{leadOver15}} reported a latest 90th percentile above 15 ppb, and {{leadOver10}} were above the coming 10 ppb level. A low utility result is good news about corrosion control — but it describes a sample, not your house.

Find out if you have a lead service line

Utilities were required to publish inventories of their service lines starting in October 2024, classifying each as lead, galvanized, non-lead or unknown, and to notify customers with lead or unknown lines. Check your utility's website or call and ask. You can also look where the water pipe enters your house, usually in the basement near the meter: lead pipe is dull gray, soft enough that scratching it with a key leaves a shiny silver mark, and a magnet won't stick to it.

Test your own tap

  • Many utilities offer free lead testing; start there.
  • Otherwise use a state-certified lab, or a {{amazon:lead water test kit lab analysis|mail-in lead test kit}} that's analyzed by a certified lab. Instant test strips are fine for spotting very high lead but not for low levels.
  • Take a "first draw" sample — water that has sat in the pipes at least six hours, usually first thing in the morning — since that's when lead is highest.

Reduce exposure now

  • Use a filter certified for lead. Look for certification to NSF/ANSI Standard 53 for lead reduction, or Standard 58 for reverse osmosis systems. Many inexpensive filters reduce only chlorine taste and odor (Standard 42). An {{amazon:NSF 53 lead certified water filter pitcher|NSF 53-certified pitcher}} or faucet filter is the cheapest option; see our filter guide.
  • Use cold water for drinking, cooking and baby formula; hot water draws more lead.
  • Run the tap for a few minutes, or until it feels colder, after water has sat for hours.
  • Clean faucet aerators, which can trap lead particles.
  • Don't boil water to remove lead — boiling concentrates it.

If you have a lead service line, ask your utility about its replacement program. Many now pay for all or most of the private side, funded partly by federal infrastructure money. Look up your city's utility and lead results from the home page.
