Two records worth checking

If your water comes from a community utility, two public records describe it:

  • The Consumer Confidence Report (CCR), also called the annual water quality report. Utilities must deliver it to customers by July 1 each year, covering the previous calendar year's testing. It's usually linked on your water bill or the utility's website.
  • EPA's Safe Drinking Water Information System (SDWIS), which records every violation a utility is cited for. That's the data behind the violation history on each city page on this site.

The report rules are changing: starting with reports issued in 2027, utilities serving more than 10,000 people must send them twice a year, and every report has to open with a plain-language summary of violations and any results above action levels.

Reading the results table

The core of every report is a table of contaminants the utility detected. For each one you'll see:

  • MCL (maximum contaminant level): the legal limit.
  • MCLG (goal): the level below which there's no known health risk. For some contaminants, including lead and arsenic, the goal is zero even though the legal limit isn't.
  • Level detected and range: usually an average and the lowest and highest results. A single high sample doesn't necessarily mean a violation; many limits are based on running annual averages.
  • Likely source: such as "byproduct of drinking water disinfection" or "erosion of natural deposits."

Lead and copper are reported differently, as a 90th-percentile result from homes the utility sampled, against an "action level" rather than an MCL. See our lead guide for what that number means.

Health-based vs monitoring violations

Not every violation means the water was unsafe. EPA flags violations as health-based when the water exceeded a legal limit (an MCL or disinfectant limit) or the utility failed to carry out required treatment. The rest are monitoring and reporting violations: a test that wasn't taken on time, a report not filed, a notice not sent.

Monitoring violations matter — if a required test wasn't done, nobody can say the water was fine that month — but they're a different kind of problem from a contaminant above its limit.

Disinfection byproducts — trihalomethanes and haloacetic acids — form when chlorine reacts with natural organic matter in the water. They're regulated for long-term exposure, so a violation usually reflects a running average over a year, not an acute hazard.

Acute vs long-term risks

Some violations require the utility to warn you within 24 hours: E. coli detections, nitrate above the limit (dangerous to infants), or a treatment failure that could let pathogens through. Those come with boil-water or do-not-drink notices. Most other limits are set for a lifetime of exposure, so an exceedance is a reason to follow up and perhaps filter, not to panic.

What the records can't tell you

  • Your own plumbing. Lead mostly enters water from service lines, solder and fixtures between the main and your faucet. Only a test of your tap shows that.
  • Unregulated contaminants. Many substances, including most PFAS until recently, aren't covered by limits, so a clean violation record says nothing about them. See our PFAS guide.
  • Private wells. About one in eight Americans drinks well water, which no federal rule covers. Well owners should test at least once a year for bacteria and nitrate, and periodically for arsenic and other local concerns.
