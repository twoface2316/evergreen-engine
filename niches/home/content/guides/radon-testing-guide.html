What radon is

Radon is a radioactive gas produced by the natural breakdown of uranium in soil and rock. It seeps into homes through cracks in foundations, gaps around pipes, sump pits and crawlspaces, and builds up indoors — usually most in basements and lower floors, and most in winter when houses are closed up. Breathed in over years, it damages lung tissue; EPA estimates it causes about 21,000 lung cancer deaths a year in the US.

What your radon zone means

EPA's Map of Radon Zones classifies every county by its predicted average indoor radon level:

  • Zone 1 — highest potential: predicted average above 4 picocuries per liter (pCi/L).
  • Zone 2 — moderate: 2 to 4 pCi/L.
  • Zone 3 — low: below 2 pCi/L.

Neighboring houses can differ several-fold, and high readings turn up in every zone. That's why EPA recommends testing every home, whatever the zone. Your city's page shows its county's zone.

How to test

  • Short-term tests run 2 to 90 days — most kits 2 to 4 days. What the result means

EPA's action level is 4 pCi/L: if your home averages that or more, fix it. EPA also recommends considering mitigation between 2 and 4 pCi/L, since no level of radon is risk-free. If a short-term test comes back at or above 4, confirm with a second short-term test or a long-term test before acting.

Mitigation and its cost

The standard fix is active soil depressurization: a pipe through the foundation slab connected to a fan that pulls radon from under the house and vents it above the roof. It typically cuts levels by more than half and often below 2 pCi/L. Sealing cracks helps but rarely works on its own.

EPA's guidance puts the average cost around $1,500, with most homes between about $900 and $3,000 depending on the foundation, the radon level and local labor. Use a mitigator certified by your state or a national radon proficiency program, and retest after the system is installed — then every two years or after major renovations.

Radon in well water

In some granite-rich areas, private wells carry dissolved radon that escapes into the air during showers and laundry. Air is still the larger source in most homes, but if you're on a well in a zone 1 area, ask a certified lab about testing your water for radon too.
